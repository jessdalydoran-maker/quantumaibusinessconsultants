Trades businesses lose jobs to slow response times because most customers requesting a quote contact more than one business at once, and the job frequently goes to whoever gets back to them first and makes booking easiest — regardless of who would have done the better job. Response speed functions as a filter before quality is ever compared.

## The comparison happens before you're in the room

When a customer needs an electrician, a plumber, or a builder, the normal pattern is to contact two or three options rather than one. That's not disloyalty — it's sensible shopping for a job that might cost real money and needs to be done properly. The problem for the trade being contacted is that this comparison starts immediately, at first contact, long before anyone's had a chance to demonstrate the quality of their actual work.

If one business replies within the hour with a clear next step, and another replies the next day, the first one has usually already won a meaningful share of the decision — sometimes the whole thing — before either has said a single word about how good the work will be.

## Why this hits trades businesses harder than most

A few things make this dynamic particularly sharp for trades specifically:

**The work is physically incompatible with answering the phone.** A shop-based business can have someone at a till who half-answers the phone between customers. A tradesperson mid-installation, mid-repair, or up a ladder genuinely cannot take a call without stopping — there's no in-between.

**Many enquiries are time-pressured by nature.** A burst pipe, a broken boiler in winter, an electrical fault — these aren't enquiries where a customer is content to wait a day for a reply. They're calling the next number if the first doesn't answer promptly, often within minutes.

**Word of mouth works in the customer's favour, not the trade's.** A customer comparing options today might ask friends or check reviews, but the immediate practical decision — who calls back first, who can come out soonest — often overrides longer-term reputation research, especially for anything urgent.

## What "slow" actually means in practice

It's worth being specific, because "slow" doesn't necessarily mean days. For urgent call-outs, a reply that takes even twenty or thirty minutes can lose the job to a competitor who answered on the first ring. For planned work — a kitchen rewire, a bathroom refit — the window is longer, but the same principle holds over a slightly longer timeframe: a quote that goes unfollowed-up for a week is often a quote that's already been forgotten by the customer, who booked with whoever kept the conversation alive.

## The two moments that decide most of this

Based on the patterns we see across trades businesses, two specific moments matter more than the rest combined:

1. **The first contact.** Whether a call is answered live, or a missed call gets an instant reply, versus silence until you're next free. This is the single biggest lever, because it's the moment the comparison against competitors is sharpest.
2. **The follow-up on a sent quote.** Whether anyone circles back on a quote that hasn't converted within a few days, versus it sitting untouched until the customer's already committed elsewhere.

Both are addressed directly by [enquiries and leads](/services/enquiries-and-leads) and [follow-ups and nurture](/services/follow-ups-and-nurture) — not because they're complicated problems, but because they're time-sensitive ones that don't fit neatly around a working day spent on tools.

## A simple way to check if this is actually costing you work

Most tradespeople have a rough sense that some calls go unanswered, but few have actually measured it. A useful exercise: for one working week, note every call that goes to voicemail or rings out, and every quote sent without a follow-up call or message within three days. At the end of the week, look at the total honestly — not to feel bad about it, but to see whether the number is small enough to ignore or large enough to be worth fixing. For most trades businesses we talk to, it's the second.

## This isn't a skills gap — it's a coverage gap

None of this reflects on the quality of the trade itself. A genuinely excellent electrician loses jobs to a mediocre one who simply answered the phone first, all the time.
